01

Context

Starting from a curated seed set of ovarian cancer driver genes, we traced documented gene–gene relationships outward into Tier 1 and Tier 2 using the published literature. Building that impact network surfaced something unexpected.

The literature describes relatively few direct gene–gene relationships in ovarian cancer — far fewer than the 2,175 actionable findings we previously extracted from the same corpus. There is a real gap between what gets formally reported as a relationship and what sits inside individual findings. That gap raises the practical question this study answers: when the structure is this sparse, which way of assembling a gene panel actually carries predictive signal for patient outcome?

The question, drawn out

Three candidate gene panels enter the same modeling pipeline; two survival outcomes come out. The only thing that changes between runs is how the gene set was built — so any difference in performance is a difference in the panels themselves.

Same cohort, three gene panels, two outcomes — compared head to head.

03

Three ways to build the panel

Each strategy encodes a different belief about where predictive signal lives: in curated biology, in data-driven structure, or in sheer prominence across the literature.

01

Literature-structured

By tier

Genes drawn from the impact network — seed, Tier 1, and Tier 2. Testing tiers separately shows whether curated, relationship-aware genes carry signal, and whether reaching deeper into the network adds information or dilutes it.

02

Clustering-derived

Dr. Lashmi’s set

Genes grouped by unsupervised clustering analysis. This asks whether data-driven co-expression structure, learned without reference to the literature, outperforms hand-curated relationships.

03

Frequency-based

Corpus prominence

The genes mentioned most often across the article corpus — a simple, popularity-driven baseline. If a curated or learned panel can’t beat this, the added structure isn’t buying much.

04

Outcomes & modeling

Survival is modeled two ways so the comparison isn’t tied to a single framing of the outcome.

Outcome A

Survivability

A binary label — does the patient survive past a defined horizon? Framed as a classification problem.

Logistic regression Regularized (L1/L2) Tree ensembles
Outcome B

Months survived

The continuous duration of survival, modeled with time-to-event methods that respect censoring.

Cox PH Random survival forest Regression on months
05

Evaluation

All three panels run through an identical protocol — same patients, same splits, same metrics — so the leaderboard reflects gene quality and nothing else.

Binary · survivability

  • AUROC and AUPRC — ranking and class-imbalance robustness
  • Accuracy & F1 at a chosen threshold
  • Calibration — are predicted risks trustworthy?

Continuous · duration

  • Harrell’s C-index — concordance of predicted vs. true ordering
  • Time-dependent AUC across horizons
  • RMSE on predicted months (where uncensored)

Fair-comparison guardrail. Hold the gene-set size constant across the three strategies — or report performance as a function of set size. Otherwise a gap could simply reflect how many genes a panel contains rather than how good those genes are. Validation uses repeated, stratified cross-validation with a held-out test set.

06

Open decisions & next steps

A handful of design choices to settle before building the pipeline — flagged here for discussion.

  • ?Cohort & data source. Confirm the patient cohort and feature modality (e.g., TCGA-OV expression and/or mutation profiles joined to clinical survival).
  • ?Binary threshold. Define “survives” — median months, or a fixed clinical horizon such as 3- or 5-year survival.
  • ?Gene-set size matching. Decide whether to equalize panel sizes across strategies, or sweep size as a variable.
  • ?A combined arm. Consider a fourth panel from the union or intersection of the three, to see whether the strategies are complementary.
